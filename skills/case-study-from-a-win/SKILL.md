# Case Study From a Win

Turn one real customer win into a case study a skeptical buyer believes. The failure mode is a glossy testimonial with no numbers, no specifics, and a quote no human would say. The other failure mode is worse: inventing the result. Do neither. A case study earns trust by being checkable, so every claim traces back to something the customer actually said or did.

## Inputs you need

Ask for these if they are missing. Do not proceed on guesses.

- **Who the customer is:** company, industry, size, and the role of the person who felt the pain.
- **The before:** the specific problem, and what it was costing them (time, money, risk, a stalled goal).
- **The change:** what they did with your product, and the one thing that actually moved the needle.
- **The after:** the result, with a real number if one exists. If there is no number yet, say so and use the concrete before/after instead.
- **Any real quote** the customer gave, in their own words. If you do not have one, leave a placeholder and do not write one for them.

## What to produce

1. **A one-line headline** built on the sharpest true result. If the result is a number, lead with it. No "how [Company] transformed their workflow" filler.
2. **The story in three beats:** the before (the pain, made specific), the change (what they did, not a feature list), and the after (what is different now). Keep it to a few tight paragraphs.
3. **A pull quote,** either the real one they gave or a clearly marked `[needs a real quote from the customer]` placeholder. Never fabricate one.
4. **The metrics block:** the two or three numbers that matter, each labeled with its source. Any number you were not given goes in `[brackets]` for the team to confirm.
5. **A one-line approval note** listing what the customer must sign off on before this ships.

## Rules

- Invent nothing. No numbers, no quotes, no outcomes the inputs do not support. If a number is missing, flag it in `[brackets]`; do not estimate one.
- Specific beats grand. "Cut onboarding from three weeks to four days" beats "dramatically accelerated time to value."
- Keep the customer the hero, not the product. The product is how they won, not the point of the story.
- No em dashes, no filler verbs, no vague superlatives. If a sentence would survive with the customer's name swapped out, it is too generic; cut it.
